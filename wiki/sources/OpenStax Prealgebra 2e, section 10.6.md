## In one sentence

Section 10.6 of OpenStax *Prealgebra 2e*, "Introduction to Factoring Polynomials": factoring
as the reverse of multiplying, "we will start with a product and then break it down into its
factors"; the greatest common factor of two or more expressions found by writing each
coefficient as a product of primes and each power in expanded form and matching the common
factors in columns, $12x^2$ and $18x^3$ giving $6x^2$; and the GCF taken out of a polynomial
by the distributive law "in reverse", $ab + ac = a(b + c)$, so $2x + 14 = 2(x + 7)$,
$14x^3 + 8x^2 - 10x = 2x(7x^2 + 4x - 5)$ and, with a negative leading coefficient,
$-9y - 27 = -9(y + 3)$, each checked by multiplying back.

## What it covers

Cite it for these claims, and for no others:

- Factoring as the reverse of multiplying: "Earlier we multiplied factors together to get a
  product. Now, we will be reversing this process; we will start with a product and then break
  it down into its factors. Splitting a product into factors is called factoring." A figure
  labels $8 \cdot 7 = 56$ and $2x(x + 3) = 2x^2 + 6x$ with *factors* and *product*, an arrow
  "multiply" to the right and an arrow "factor" to the left. Serves [[Factorisation]] and
  [[Factors and multiples]].
- *Factor* as noun and verb, named as such in the text: "$7$ is a factor of $14$" against
  "factor $2$ from $2x + 14$". Serves [[Factors and multiples]] and [[Factorisation]].
- The greatest common factor defined: "The greatest common factor (GCF) of two or more
  expressions is the largest expression that is a factor of all the expressions." Serves
  [[Factors and multiples]] and [[Common factors, quadratics, and difference of squares]].
- The GCF of two numbers by prime factorisation, with factor trees: $24 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2
  \cdot 3$ and $36 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 3$, the common $2$, $2$, $3$ circled in columns
  and multiplied, so the GCF of $24$ and $36$ is $12$; then "since the GCF is a factor of both
  numbers, $24$ and $36$ can be written as multiples of $12$", $24 = 12 \cdot 2$ and
  $36 = 12 \cdot 3$. The method is said to be "similar to what we used to find the LCM".
  Serves [[Factors and multiples]].
- The four-step procedure for the GCF of expressions: factor each coefficient into primes and
  "write all variables with exponents in expanded form"; list the factors "matching common
  factors in a column" and circle them; "bring down the common factors that all expressions
  share"; multiply them. Examples: $5x$ and $15$ give $5$; $12x^2$ and $18x^3$ give $6x^2$;
  $14x^3$, $8x^2$ and $10x$ give $2x$. Serves
  [[Common factors, quadratics, and difference of squares]] and, for the GCF of a constant
  with a term, [[Factors and multiples]].
- The distributive law stated in both directions, for real numbers $a$, $b$, $c$:
  $a(b + c) = ab + ac$ and $ab + ac = a(b + c)$; "The form on the left is used to multiply.
  The form on the right is used to factor." Factoring is the Distributive Property applied
  "in reverse", and $2(x + 7)$ is shown becoming $2 \cdot x + 2 \cdot 7$ and then $2x + 14$,
  labelled *factors* and *product*. Serves [[Distributive law, expansion, and like terms]]
  and [[Common factors, quadratics, and difference of squares]].
- Factored form as a representation of the same thing: "where it is sometimes useful to
  represent a number in factored form (for example, $12$ as $2 \cdot 6$ or $3 \cdot 4$), in
  algebra it can be useful to represent a polynomial in factored form." Serves
  [[Factorisation]].
- The four-step procedure for taking the GCF out of a polynomial: find the GCF of all the
  terms; "rewrite each term as a product using the GCF"; "use the Distributive Property 'in
  reverse' to factor the expression"; "check by multiplying the factors". Worked:
  $2x + 14 = 2 \cdot x + 2 \cdot 7 = 2(x + 7)$; $3a + 3 = 3 \cdot a + 3 \cdot 1 = 3(a + 1)$,
  where the term equal to the GCF is rewritten with a factor $1$; $12x - 60 = 12(x - 5)$, with
  the reminder "to write the GCF as the product of all the common factors"; a trinomial,
  $3y^2 + 6y + 9 = 3(y^2 + 2y + 3)$; a variable as the GCF, $6x^2 + 5x = x(6x + 5)$, checked
  as $x \cdot 6x + x \cdot 5 = 6x^2 + 5x$; $4x^3 - 20x^2 = 4x^2(x - 5)$;
  $21y^2 + 35y = 7y(3y + 5)$; and $14x^3 + 8x^2 - 10x = 2x(7x^2 + 4x - 5)$. Serves
  [[Common factors, quadratics, and difference of squares]] and, for the check,
  [[Distributive law, expansion, and like terms]].
- A negative leading coefficient: "When the leading coefficient, the coefficient of the first
  term, is negative, we factor the negative out as part of the GCF." The GCF is found
  "ignoring the signs of the terms" and then made negative: $-9y - 27 = -9 \cdot y + (-9)
  \cdot 3 = -9(y + 3)$ and $-4a^2 + 16a = -4a \cdot a - (-4a) \cdot 4 = -4a(a - 4)$, with
  "Pay close attention to the signs of the terms." Serves
  [[Common factors, quadratics, and difference of squares]].

The section has **no quadratic factorisation**: its trinomials, $3y^2 + 6y + 9$ and
$7x^2 + 4x - 5$ inside the bracket, are only ever factored by a common factor, and nothing in
it writes a quadratic as a product of two binomials. It has **no difference of squares**:
$a^2 - b^2 = (a + b)(a - b)$ appears nowhere, and the only binomials with two squares,
$4a^3 - 4ab^2$ and $6c^3 - 6cd^2$ in the exercises, are set only for their GCF. A Note must
not cite it for either. It also holds a readiness quiz ("Be Prepared") on factoring $56$ into
primes and multiplying $-3(6a + 11)$ and $4x^2(x^2 + 3x - 1)$, a passing reference to the
least common multiple found earlier in the book, three "Media" links to videos, the numbered
exercise set, "Everyday Math" exercises on a revenue polynomial $-5p^2 + 150p$ and the height
of a baseball $-16t^2 + 80t + 4$, writing exercises (one asking for a general rule for the
GCF of $y^a$, $y^b$ and $y^c$, which the text never states) and a self-check. No Node needs
them, so no Note should cite the section for them.

## Notation it uses

Each entry below records how this source writes the convention, attributed to it:

- [[Conventions#Factorise and highest common factor]]: *factor* as the verb, "Splitting a
  product into factors is called factoring", with *Factor:* as the instruction and *factor the
  greatest common factor from a polynomial* for taking it out; *greatest common factor*,
  abbreviated *GCF*, where this Wiki's proposal says *highest common factor* and *HCF*;
  *factored form* for the result; and *least common multiple (LCM)* in its one backward
  reference, where UK usage is *lowest common multiple*.
- [[Conventions#Distributive law]]: *the Distributive Property*, capitalised, stated in both
  directions, $a(b + c) = ab + ac$ "used to multiply" and $ab + ac = a(b + c)$ "used to
  factor", and applied "in reverse" to factor; *multiply* for the step that removes brackets,
  "Check by multiplying the factors", with *distribute* not appearing in its prose.
- [[Conventions#Multiplication sign]]: a centred dot between numbers, $24 = 12 \cdot 2$, and
  between a number or monomial and what it multiplies when a term is rewritten as a product,
  $2 \cdot x + 2 \cdot 7$ and $x \cdot 6x + x \cdot 5$; no sign between a number and a letter,
  $2x$, $6x^2$ and $14x^3$, with the number written first; a number or monomial against a
  bracket with no sign, $2(x + 7)$, $x(6x + 5)$ and $-4a(a - 4)$; and, in its figures' `alt`
  text only, the letter x and an asterisk as the sign, "2 x 2 x 2 x 3", "5*x" and "3*3*y".
- [[Conventions#Brackets]]: it writes *parentheses* for $(\,)$, once, in a figure's `alt`
  text, "2x times parentheses x plus 3"; the word does not appear in its prose.
- [[Conventions#Index and power]]: *exponent* for the index, "Write all variables with
  exponents in expanded form", so $x^3$ is written $x \cdot x \cdot x$ before factors are
  matched; and "variables raised to powers".
- [[Conventions#Term, coefficient and constant]]: *term*, "the GCF of all the terms of the
  polynomial"; *coefficient*, "Factor each coefficient into primes", and *leading coefficient*
  defined as "the coefficient of the first term"; *constants* for bare numbers, "the greatest
  common factor of constants"; and *polynomial*, *monomial*, *binomial* and *trinomial* for
  expressions by their number of terms, "multiply a polynomial by a monomial", "factor a
  variable from a binomial", "factor the greatest common factor from a trinomial", words this
  Wiki has not adopted.
- [[Conventions#The negative of an expression]]: *the negative*, "we factor the negative out
  as part of the GCF", and "the GCF will be negative", for $-9$ in $-9(y + 3)$ and $-4a$ in
  $-4a(a - 4)$.
- [[Conventions#Equation, solution and solve]]: a check of a factorisation, not of a
  solution, "Check by multiplying the factors", written as a column of expressions with a tick
  after the last, $x(6x + 5)$, then $x \cdot 6x + x \cdot 5$, then $6x^2 + 5x$, with no
  equals sign and no question mark; most checks are images whose `alt` text says "confirmed
  with a checkmark".

*Find the greatest common factor* and *Factor* are its instructions; *Multiply* is the
instruction of its readiness quiz. It writes *prime* and *factor tree* for the prime
factorisation of a number, and *expanded form* for a power written out as repeated factors.
No division, decimal, fraction or long number appears in the section, so it shows nothing for
the entries on division, cancelling, division restrictions or digit grouping. Variables are
single italic letters, and the letters in the distributive law are $a$, $b$, $c$. Most of its
worked solutions are images, with the expression described in the image's `alt` text, and the
circled common factors are visible only there. It spells in American English (*summarize*),
which is spelling, not notation: a Note citing it still writes British English.
